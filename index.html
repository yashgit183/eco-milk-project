01 / Design brief

The proposed solution

Use paperboard where it performs well, and a thin certified barrier liner where liquid containment and food safety demand it.

01 · COST

Simple converting

Die-cut and fold a paperboard sleeve around a separately formed liner. The student build uses hand tools; commercial production could use carton converting and pouch-sealing equipment.

02 · MATERIAL

Fibre-forward structure

Paperboard supplies stiffness, printability and opacity. The liner is kept thin and removable in principle, reducing the amount of polymer compared with a thick rigid bottle.

03 · PROTECTION

Barrier-led performance

The sealed liner contains the milk. The opaque shell reduces light exposure. An EVOH-containing coextruded liner may improve oxygen barrier, subject to supplier data and validation.

Design decision: The most realistic college prototype is for refrigerated pasteurised milk. It is not an aseptic, shelf-stable pack. Aseptic milk requires validated sterilisation of product, package and filling environment, plus a much stronger oxygen/light barrier system.
02 / Technical architecture

Layer-by-layer construction

Food contact is isolated to a commercially sourced, documented liner. Printed board, adhesives and craft coatings never touch the milk.

Exploded concept: the paperboard sleeve is secondary packaging; the sealed flexible liner is the primary liquid barrier.

03 / Student build

Build the 500 mL demonstration model

Make the outer shell from card and the inner pouch from a commercially supplied food-contact film pouch. Use coloured water for all student demonstrations.

Target dimensions

Approximate pack envelope

70 × 55 × 170 mm

Nominal target: 500 mL. Actual filled dimensions depend on pouch gusset, headspace and board allowance. Prototype can be slightly larger to fit a 500 mL water-filled liner.

Suggested shell blank: 250 × 190 mm including overlap and fold allowances. Make a paper mock-up first and adjust after test-fitting the pouch.

What you need

Materials & tools

  • 300–400 gsm paperboard / thick kraft card, approx. A3 sheet
  • Supplier-sourced food-contact PE/EVOH/PE pouch or pre-made barrier pouch (do not fabricate food-contact film)
  • Food-grade-compatible impulse sealer for sealing pouch edges, if the film supplier specifies suitable settings
  • Ruler, steel scale, pencil, cutter, cutting mat, scissors
  • Bone folder / blunt scoring tool, clips, double-sided tape for outer shell only
  • 500 mL measuring jug, water, food colouring, tissue paper

Assembly sequence

Prepare the liner

Use a ready-made, documented food-contact pouch. If supplied as film, only seal it using the supplier's validated temperature, pressure and dwell-time guidance. Do not improvise a milk-contact seal.

Leak-check the empty pouch

Fill with 500 mL coloured water, seal, wipe dry, then place on absorbent tissue for 10 minutes. Inspect corners and seals. This is a demonstration check, not a food-safety validation.

Cut the paperboard blank

Mark a wrap-around sleeve with front, back and two side panels. Score fold lines before cutting. Leave a 12–15 mm overlap tab; add top and bottom retaining flaps.

Fold the outer shell

Fold along the score lines using a ruler. Join the overlap tab with tape or adhesive on the board exterior / non-food-contact side. Keep all adhesive away from the liner.

Insert the sealed pouch

Lower the filled, sealed liner into the sleeve. Avoid sharp board edges. Add a folded card base insert to support the pouch and prevent it slipping out.

Make the opening feature

For the student version, use a tear-open top on the outer shell and pour from the sealed pouch after cutting above the seal. A screw cap requires a professionally fitted food-grade spout and validated seal.

Brand and label

Print the ECO-MILK graphics on the outside. Mark “DEMO MODEL – NOT FOR MILK STORAGE”, nominal capacity, material layers and refrigeration concept.

Demonstrate and document

Photograph front, side, top and opened assembly. Record dimensions, liner mass, board mass, seal observations and test conditions.

Safe demo rule: Fill with coloured water only. Do not store or serve milk from a hand-built prototype. Commercial milk filling must be hygienic, mechanically filled and sealed immediately after heat treatment under applicable requirements.

04 / Validation plan

Test what the design actually proves

Use a repeatable comparison protocol. Report measured observations and avoid claiming a shelf-life increase until a qualified lab has validated it.

Physical package tests

  1. Leakage: Fill with coloured water, dry exterior, place on pre-weighed absorbent paper for 10–30 min; record visible leakage and mass change. Repeat n=3.
  2. Compression / stacking: Apply incremental known loads to filled demo packs; record deformation, seam failure and recovery. Keep test load consistent.
  3. Drop: Drop water-filled demo packs from a fixed height and orientation onto a defined surface; inspect seal and corners. Use a safety tray.
  4. Light protection: Place equal volumes of a light-sensitive indicator or coloured model liquid in clear pouch, opaque shell and reference carton; expose to a controlled light source for fixed intervals. This is a comparative demonstration, not milk-quality proof.
  5. Moisture: Weigh dry board coupons, expose to controlled humidity / water splash conditions, reweigh and record mass gain and visible softening.

Barrier and material assessment

  • Oxygen transmission rate (OTR): Request supplier test certificate or use a qualified lab method such as ASTM D3985 / ISO 15105-2, with stated temperature and relative humidity.
  • Water vapour transmission rate (WVTR): Supplier data or ASTM F1249 / ISO 15106 method under specified conditions.
  • Seal integrity: Dye penetration / bubble leak screening can identify gross defects; qualified packaging lab should conduct validated seal-strength and leak tests.
  • Light transmission: Use a spectrophotometer / light transmission measurement across visible and UV ranges if available.
  • Environmental comparison: Compare grams of each material per 500 mL, recyclability in the local collection system, transport weight and end-of-life route. A full LCA needs defined functional unit and system boundary.

Milk shelf-life study — laboratory only

To investigate shelf life, a food microbiology laboratory should compare the proposed package with a commercial reference using the same milk batch, processing conditions, fill temperature, hygienic filling controls, storage temperature and sampling schedule. Include enough independent packs at each time point, defined in the approved protocol.

Measurements to consider

  • Total viable count / standard plate count
  • Coliforms and relevant hygiene indicators
  • Psychrotrophic count for chilled storage
  • pH and titratable acidity
  • Sensory assessment only under approved safe protocol
  • Visual changes, swelling, odour and package integrity

Controls and reporting

  • Use validated methods, sterile sampling and documented chain of custody
  • Maintain calibrated cold storage and continuous temperature logs
  • Set microbiological acceptance limits with the lab / applicable standards
  • Do not taste test suspect or incubated samples
  • Stop at failure criteria; dispose as biohazard / lab waste per SOP
  • Report shelf-life only from statistically supported results
Important: Packaging alone does not make milk safe. Shelf life depends on heat treatment, initial microbial load, hygienic filling, package integrity, oxygen/light exposure and uninterrupted cold-chain temperature. This design is not aseptic.

06 / Report writing kit

Summer internship report structure

Use the sections below as the report framework. Add your own measured data, photographs, test sheets and supervisor feedback.

Abstract

This project explores a low-cost, fibre-forward packaging concept for chilled milk. A paperboard outer sleeve is combined with a commercially sourced food-contact barrier liner to provide structural support, light shielding and liquid containment. The prototype is developed as a student demonstration model and assessed through physical package tests, material comparison and a proposed laboratory shelf-life protocol. No shelf-life improvement is claimed without experimental validation.

Problem statement & objectives

Conventional milk packs must balance affordability, food safety, product protection and end-of-life impact. The project investigates whether a separable paperboard shell and thin barrier liner can provide a practical alternative concept while maintaining reliable containment.

  • Develop a low-cost prototype.
  • Evaluate material suitability and packaging functions.
  • Compare physical performance with common formats.
  • Propose a scientifically valid shelf-life study.

Introduction & literature review

Review milk deterioration factors (microbial growth, oxygen, light and temperature), packaging barrier functions, paperboard liquid-packaging structures, flexible coextruded films, EVOH oxygen barrier, compostable polymers and packaging end-of-life systems. Distinguish chilled pasteurised milk from aseptic UHT milk.

Methodology & prototype development

Document design requirements, material sourcing, dimensions, drawing, cutting and folding, liner selection, sealing conditions, assembly photographs, bill of materials and all deviations from the planned design.

Testing & results

Present leakage, drop / compression, light exposure and moisture observations in tables. Include sample count, test conditions, equipment, photographs and limitations. Mark all values as measured, supplier-declared or estimated.

Cost, limitations & future scope

Include prototype cost and a separate hypothetical scale-up cost model. Limitations include no commercial line trial, no certified migration testing, no full LCA and no validated shelf-life data. Future work: mono-material barrier research, closure development, pilot filling trials and independent LCA.

Expected conclusion (edit after testing)

The prototype demonstrates a practical way to combine a renewable paperboard outer structure with a dedicated liquid barrier liner. It may improve handling stiffness and reduce direct light exposure compared with an exposed transparent pouch, while keeping the liner thin. Its environmental benefit is not automatic: it depends on material mass, liner separation, collection infrastructure and actual recycling outcomes. Shelf-life performance remains to be established through controlled laboratory testing.

Final engineering recommendation

Why this is a credible student project

ECO-MILK is buildable with accessible board, a documented barrier pouch, hand tools and shared sealing equipment. It demonstrates a meaningful packaging-engineering trade-off: use fibre for structure and light shielding, and reserve polymer for the functions that paper cannot safely perform.

For the final evaluation, present it honestly as a prototype for chilled milk packaging, not as a validated commercial package. Your strongest engineering contribution will be the material rationale, the safe construction approach, the test protocol and transparent reporting of measured results.
